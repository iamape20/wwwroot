How to Read This Page

A plain explanation of every number, badge and label on Elite Power Ratings — written honestly, including where the model has no edge.

Everything on this site comes from a statistical model built from public form, class, going, connections and market data. It is a tool for organising information, not a prediction of the future. Nothing here guarantees a winner.

The EPR Rating

Every horse gets a single EPR score, roughly 0–100, combining four things in a fixed blend: recent Form, career Context (official rating, wins, freshness), the current Market price, and a short written Verdict. The horse with the highest EPR in a race is shown as the top pick.

What the number does not mean: EPR is not a win probability and a higher number does not reliably mean a stronger chance of winning. Across every race checked to date, a horse rated under 30 has won about as often as one rated 75+. The rating tells you how the model ranks the field, not how likely any single horse is to win.

Race Separation: Strong / Moderate / Open

Every race is also tagged by how clearly the top pick stands out from the second-best horse, relative to the spread of the whole field. This is a measure of clarity, not of value.

Tag What it means
Strong The top pick is clearly separated from the rest of the field on rating.
Moderate A real but smaller gap between the top pick and the field.
Open The field is closely rated — no pick stands out.

The honest part

Strong and Moderate races have historically produced more winners than Open ones — a real, checked pattern. But the betting market prices Strong and Moderate picks accurately too: on average they return almost exactly what their odds imply, once the bookmaker's margin is accounted for. A Strong tag means the model is clearer about who's best — it does not mean the price on offer is generous, or that the bet is safe. Open races are where the model has the least to say.

Selection Badges

MARKET SELECTION

Historical results showed that, more often than not, the betting market's own favourite outperforms the model's own top-rated horse. Where that applies, the board shows the market favourite as the pick instead of the model's top EPR — clearly labelled, so you always know which one you're looking at.

EPR SELECTION

The opposite case — no reliable market favourite could be identified for this race, so the model's own top-rated horse is shown instead.

⚠️ Close call — less certain than usual

Shown when the top two horses are rated within 3 points of each other. Checked against real results: races like this genuinely produce fewer favourite-following winners than usual (roughly 15% vs 26%) — but that extra uncertainty is spread across the whole field, not concentrated in the second-placed horse specifically. Read it as "this race is harder to call," not "watch out for this one horse."

⬇ WELL TREATED

This horse is currently rated below the best official mark it has ever won off — in racing terms, it may be "well treated" by the handicapper relative to its known form.

Confidence %

Shown under each runner's name. This reflects how much reliable data the model had to work with for that horse (enough recent runs, a settled market price, and so on) — it is a measure of the model's own certainty in its inputs, not a probability of winning.

"Show scoring" reveals the four components that actually set the EPR number — Form, Context, Market, Verdict. The checklist chips above are a separate, supporting reference; they only ever break an exact tie and don't change the published rating.

Best Opportunity

The single race of the day, if any, that meets the strictest bar: Strong tier and a rating gap of 10 points or more over the second horse. Most days will have one; some days genuinely won't, and the page says so plainly rather than lowering the bar to always show something.

What this site is not

This is not a tipping service and does not promise profit. The tiers above describe how clear a race looks to the model, not whether backing the top pick is a good bet at the price on offer. Treat every rating as one input among many, use your own judgement, and never bet more than you can afford to lose.
